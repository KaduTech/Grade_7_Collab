Your science team challenge

Your team is a group of ecologists and scientific investigators. You will use evidence, models and data to explain how organisms interact, how populations change, and why inherited traits may become more common over generations.

Lessons you will use: Biological Interactions · Limiting Factors · Evidence of Change · Natural Selection.

You have been assigned to one of four groups. Find your group below and complete its specific investigation. You do not need to complete the other groups’ projects.

Your overall goal: Explain a scientific problem, show evidence, make an original visual or model, and give a convincing conclusion. Your teacher will ask questions to check that every team member understands the science.

Your teacher will confirm your team members, submission location and exact due date. Do not assume a due date from this guide.

Group 1 · Food-web investigators

Ecosystem Under Threat

Your situation: A forest ecosystem experiences a sudden decline in one predator species. The other populations begin to change, but the outcome is not immediately obvious.

Your mission: Model the ecosystem and explain at least two possible chains of effects after predator numbers fall.

Investigation tasks — complete all five

  1. Build a food web with at least eight organisms, including producers, consumers and decomposers.
  2. Use arrows in the direction of energy transfer (food → eater).
  3. Label and explain at least three biological interactions, such as predation, competition or mutualism.
  4. Predict immediate and later effects of the predator decline; explain why the results may be uncertain.
  5. Recommend a conservation response and support it with scientific reasoning.
Your science model or visual: An original food-web diagram with at least eight organisms, correctly directed arrows, and highlighted changes after predator decline.

Research evidence: Use at least one credible ecosystem case study or evidence-based explanation of predator–prey relationships.

Challenge question: Could removing a predator eventually harm the prey population it once hunted? Describe a possible sequence of events. Prepare a reasoned answer for your presentation.

Group 2 · Population scientists

The Population Survival Experiment

Your situation: Rabbits are introduced to an island. Their population grows, but food, water and breeding space are limited; later a drought affects the island.

Your mission: Investigate limiting factors and carrying capacity using a clearly labeled simulated ten-year dataset.

Investigation tasks — complete all five

  1. Define limiting factors and carrying capacity in your own words.
  2. Create plausible hypothetical rabbit counts for ten consecutive years; label the data as simulated, not real observations.
  3. Plot population against year and label both axes and the units.
  4. Explain how drought, disease and food shortages could affect survival and population growth.
  5. Recommend one evidence-based way to manage the habitat sustainably and state a limitation.
Your science model or visual: A graph of your ten-year simulated population with important changes annotated and a short explanation of the assumed conditions.

Research evidence: Cite a trustworthy source that explains carrying capacity or density-dependent and density-independent factors.

Challenge question: Why can a population decrease even when there are no predators? Prepare a reasoned answer for your presentation.

Group 3 · Fossil researchers

The Evolution Detectives

Your situation: Scientists find fossils from different rock layers with similar features but noticeable differences in body structures.

Your mission: Investigate what fossil evidence can—and cannot—tell scientists about change in populations over time.

Investigation tasks — complete all five

  1. Choose a documented fossil sequence or well-researched example of change over time.
  2. Compare the features of at least two organisms or fossil forms and identify similarities and differences.
  3. Place the examples on a timeline using supported approximate ages or a reliable ordering.
  4. Explain how the evidence supports change over generations; separate observations from interpretations.
  5. Identify one uncertainty or limitation in the fossil evidence.
Your science model or visual: A fossil evidence board or timeline with labeled comparisons, pictures or drawings, dates where supported, and source credits.

Research evidence: Use a museum, university or reputable scientific source for your fossil examples and time information.

Challenge question: How can scientists tell the difference between evidence of evolutionary change and ordinary differences among individuals? Prepare a reasoned answer for your presentation.

Group 4 · Evolution modelers

Natural Selection: Changing Environments

Your situation: A population of insects contains different inherited colors. Over several generations, a once-green habitat becomes darker, changing how easily birds can find the insects.

Your mission: Use a repeatable simulation to test how camouflage and inherited differences may affect survival and reproduction.

Investigation tasks — complete all five

  1. Explain inherited variation and why not all insects begin with the same color.
  2. Predict which insects predators may spot most easily on light and dark backgrounds.
  3. Create a simulation using colored paper insects, counters, a spreadsheet or another simple method; keep the rules consistent.
  4. Run at least five generations and record the number of each color after each generation.
  5. Graph the results and explain whether they support your prediction; identify one limitation of your simulation.
Your science model or visual: A table and graph showing population counts across at least five generations, plus a photo or diagram of your simulation setup.

Research evidence: Use a reliable science source explaining inherited variation, differential survival and reproduction.

Challenge question: When the habitat changes color, does each individual insect change its inherited color automatically? Why not? Prepare a reasoned answer for your presentation.

How to complete your project

Follow these steps

  1. Meet your team. Write down each member’s name, agree on your group challenge and select roles.
  2. Plan the investigation. Decide what information you need, what you will create and who will do each task.
  3. Research reliable sources. Compare information, take notes in your own words and save source details.
  4. Create a scientific model or visual. Include labels, arrows, legends and units where needed; your model must explain the problem.
  5. Analyze and conclude. Connect the evidence to your findings; say what you predict and why.
  6. Prepare and rehearse. Create 5–7 slides, practise speaking and be ready for the challenge question.

Exactly what your group must submit

  1. One presentation (5–7 slides). Include the project title and members, the problem, key science ideas, evidence, the model or visual, and your conclusion.
  2. One original scientific model or visual. This may be a diagram, map, graph, simulation or photo of a safe physical model. It can be embedded in the slides or attached separately.
  3. One research summary (200–300 words). Explain your question, method or research approach, strongest evidence, key findings, conclusion and any important limitation.
  4. One group contribution record. List every member’s name, role, tasks completed and how the team collaborated.
  5. At least two credible references. Give each source’s title, author or organization if available, and a usable link or publication details. Credit images and data too.
Recommended slide order: 1. Title & group · 2. Problem & question · 3. Science explanation · 4. Evidence & findings · 5. Model or data · 6. Solution or conclusion · 7. References / limitations (if needed).
